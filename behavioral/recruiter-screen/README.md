## Problem

This is the first round of the process: a 30-minute call with a recruiter, not an engineer. A resume
walkthrough is brief or skipped outright — the call opens directly on why you want to work at Anthropic,
and a substantial share of the remaining time goes to AI safety and Anthropic's mission, at the depth of a
first conversation rather than a full technical or values round. The recruiter also lays out the shape of
the rest of the loop and what a reference check will require. Passing this call moves you forward, but it
does not always schedule a technical round directly: a separate review of your background by the hiring
manager can sit between this call and the first technical interview. The questions fall into four groups.

### Motivation

- Why Anthropic, specifically — not just an AI company, but this one, rather than another lab working on
  similar problems?
- Why do you want to work at an AI company at all, given where your career has been so far?
- Why are you looking for a new role right now, and why leave your current one?
- If you end up with more than one offer, what is your decision framework for choosing between them?
- What long-term career direction are you aiming for?

### Safety and mission

- What does AI safety mean to you, in your own words?
- How would you describe what sets Anthropic apart from other AI labs — in mission, research focus,
  product choices, or approach to safety?
- What do you see as the biggest risks and the biggest benefits of more capable AI, and what could go
  wrong if a system like that is misused?
- Name one AI-related problem you think is currently underrated.
- Point to one specific, recent piece of Anthropic's public work you have actually read — a paper, a blog
  post, a policy document such as the Responsible Scaling Policy — and say what you took from it.

### Background

- Walk through your background and your most recent or most relevant project.
- Which of your past projects — backend, infrastructure, distributed systems — line up best with what
  this role does, and how much hands-on experience do you have building that kind of system?
- For the project you consider most relevant: what was your part in it end to end, and how long did it
  take?
- If your title implies leading people (tech lead, manager), what does the role actually involve day to
  day, and — if the role you're applying for is an individual-contributor one — are you comfortable with
  that scope?

### Logistics

- What is your work-authorization status, and what location or locations can you work from?
- Which programming language would you use in the technical interviews?
- When could you start interviewing, and, separately, start the job if offered? What other interview
  processes are you in, at what stage, and do any of them have a decision deadline?
- What is your current level, what level are you targeting here, and what are your compensation
  expectations?
- Is there a specific kind of team or work you're hoping to be matched with, if the team isn't decided by
  this call?

## Reference solution

<details>
<summary>Show the reference solution</summary>

Time in this call skews toward the first two groups above: keep your background and logistics answers
short and factual, and leave most of the thirty minutes for motivation and safety. A one-sentence answer
to a safety question is not sufficient on its own — have a second, more concrete sentence ready, with a
specific mechanism or example, since the topic is meant to be explored rather than checked off.

### Motivation

What it probes: whether your reason for choosing Anthropic is specific to Anthropic and to you, rather
than a sentence that would work at any AI company. An answer that stays at the level of Anthropic's own
public description of itself — strong mission, strong research — reads as polished but impersonal, even
when it is sincerely meant.

Two-minute skeleton for "why Anthropic": about 20 seconds naming the one thing about Anthropic's work — a
specific product, research direction, or published position — you actually hold an opinion on, not just
admiration for; about 30 seconds connecting that to a concrete piece of your own history — a project, a
decision, or the reason you moved into the field you're in — that shows the interest predates this
interview; about 40 seconds on what you would actually do in the role and why that is where you would
apply the thing you named first; about 30 seconds on why now — the specific change in your situation or
thinking that makes this the moment, stated as a fact about you rather than a complaint about your current
employer. Keep the whole answer under two minutes: longer starts to read as rehearsed rather than
considered.

For "why an AI company at all," name the specific capability or problem that changed your calculus, tied
to something you have directly built, used, or seen fail — not "AI is the future." For "why leave / why
now," state the reason as what you are moving toward, translating any real dissatisfaction into the kind
of work or environment you are seeking rather than narrating the complaint. For the multiple-offers
decision framework, name two or three criteria you would actually weigh, in a stated priority order, and
be honest that compensation is one of them rather than pretending it isn't. For long-term direction, state
the trajectory in one sentence and connect this specific role to one step of it.

Most common way to lose points: a "why Anthropic" answer that is accurate about Anthropic but says nothing
about you, or a "why now" that reads as a list of grievances about your current employer. Fix: before the
call, write down the one thing you actually have an opinion on and the one piece of your own history that
backs it up, and rehearse the two-minute version until it fits without rushing.

### Safety and mission

What it probes: whether you have already formed your own specific view, with at least one concrete
anchor, rather than something assembled on the spot — not yet the fully pressure-tested version that a
later, dedicated conversation about safety judgment will ask for.

For "what AI safety means to you," give a definition in two parts: the class of harm you are most
concerned about — for example a model being used to deceive or to cause financial or physical harm, or a
model behaving in ways its own developers did not intend and cannot fully verify — and the kind of work
that reduces it, naming the one closest to what you would actually do (evaluation, interpretability,
alignment training, deployment policy). Anchor it with one sentence from your own work that resembles this
even loosely, such as not shipping until an assumption was verified, or catching a failure mode that would
have reached whoever used the system downstream.

For "what sets Anthropic apart," name one concrete, checkable fact — the Responsible Scaling Policy, a
specific piece of published safety research, a public governance choice — rather than a general "they care
more about safety," and say why that particular fact is what convinced you.

For risks and benefits, name one specific risk with a mechanism (who could misuse what capability, toward
what end) and one specific benefit you actually believe in, at a level of detail that shows you have
thought about how each would actually happen, not just the headline version.

For the underrated problem, name one with a reason it is underrated — for instance that it is not dramatic
enough to be newsworthy, or that it accumulates gradually rather than announcing itself as a single
incident — rather than simply a less familiar term.

For the reaction to Anthropic's public work, name the specific document, post, or paper, state its core
claim in your own words, and give a genuine reaction specific enough that it could not be said about
something you had not actually read; a point of real, narrow disagreement reads as more prepared than
blanket agreement with everything.

Most common way to lose points: an abstract, one-line answer ("safety is important to me") with nothing
specific attached, language borrowed from Anthropic's own public materials with no personal anchor, or
having nothing further ready if the first sentence is not enough. Fix: before the call, read one or two
pieces of Anthropic's own safety material closely enough to react to them, and write out, in your own
words, one specific answer to each of the five questions above, so the concrete version is what comes out
rather than something invented in the moment.

### Background

What it probes: whether you can present your own experience concisely for a listener who will not follow
technical detail, and specifically how directly it lines up with the kind of work this role does — this
call is a background-fit check as much as a values check, even though it gets less time than motivation
and safety.

Skeleton for the walkthrough: your current role in one sentence, then the single most relevant project —
what you built, your own part in it end to end, how long it took, and its outcome — then one sentence
stating plainly how much hands-on experience you have with the specific kind of system this role builds,
such as backend services, infrastructure, or distributed systems, rather than leaving the interviewer to
extrapolate it from a job title. If your title suggests you lead people, state the actual scope in one
sentence: headcount, whether you still write code, and roughly how your time splits; if the role you are
applying for is an individual-contributor one, say plainly whether you are comfortable with that and why,
rather than leaving it for the interviewer to infer.

Most common way to lose points: narrating a full career history instead of the one project that matches
the role, or leaving the connection between your experience and the specific kind of work this role does
implicit. Fix: before the call, decide which one project's scale and shape most resembles what the team
builds, and have the one-sentence version of why it maps to the role ready, rather than assuming the
connection is obvious from your resume.

### Logistics

What it probes: not a single correct answer, but whether your constraints are clear and workable enough
for the rest of the process to be scheduled around them.

**Work authorization and location.** State your status and location flexibility plainly and early; a
constraint raised now costs less than the same constraint discovered after later rounds have been
scheduled around an assumption that turns out to be wrong.

**Language for interviews.** Python is the default the interview loop is built around; naming a different
language — Java or C++, for example — is fine, but say so explicitly rather than assuming the interviewer
will accommodate it, since it needs to be confirmed with whoever runs the technical rounds.

**Timeline and other processes.** State your real interview availability and, separately, your notice
period if one applies, plainly. Name the stage your other processes are at and any decision deadline they
have given you; there is no need to name which companies, since the stage and the deadline are what is
needed to coordinate scheduling.

**Compensation.** This is asked to check that a number is workable, not to find your true minimum. If the
level and the pay structure for the role — the split between base, equity, and any other components, and
how it scales by level — have not come up yet, ask about them before giving a number; it is also fair to
ask what the role is budgeted at. Once you know the level being discussed, give a range rather than a
single figure, say plainly whether it refers to base or total compensation, and base the range on research
into the market for that role and level — public compensation data, or people in comparable roles — rather
than on what you currently make, which is information about your last negotiation, not about what this
role is worth. Pair the range with your current level and the level you believe you are targeting here,
offered as your own assessment rather than a demand, since the recruiter is also using it to check where
you would plausibly land.

**Team preferences.** If you have a genuine preference for the kind of team or work — research-adjacent,
product, infrastructure — say so plainly, and ask whether the role is tied to a specific team yet or
decided later. If you are later matched to a team that turns out not to fit your background, it is fine
to ask to be re-routed; raise it as soon as you notice the mismatch rather than after later rounds have
been scheduled around it.

### Questions worth asking the recruiter

- What is the format and length of the remaining rounds, and how many are there?
- Is the loop tied to a specific team already, or does team matching happen later — and if later, at what
  point in the process?
- What level is the role being scoped at?
- Does a separate hiring-manager review of your background happen before a technical round is scheduled,
  or does passing this call schedule one directly?
- What should you read or prepare before the next round?
