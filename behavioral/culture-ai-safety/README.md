## Problem

This is a 55-minute conversation with one interviewer, one of the rounds in the onsite loop. Nothing is
submitted beforehand. What you bring is a specific, considered answer to why you want to work at Anthropic
and on AI safety, a decision process that holds up as a hypothetical dilemma is pushed further with
follow-up questions, a defensible opinion — including real disagreement — on the company's own published
safety commitments, a set of personal stories with concrete stakes, and a separate read on your character
and judgment, including one prompt argued live in the room. The questions fall into five groups.

### Motivation and mission

- Why Anthropic, specifically — as opposed to another lab or company working on related problems?
- Which one of Anthropic's stated values resonates with you personally, and why — with a concrete example
  of when you acted on it, not merely admired it?
- Why does AI safety matter to you personally, rather than as a cause that would fit any AI employer?
- How does your past work — the specific projects, decisions, or field you came from — actually connect to
  that concern?
- What would make you want to leave Anthropic?
- What could Anthropic do better?

### Hypothetical dilemmas

- Which risk from more capable AI do you personally take most seriously, and what evidence would change
  your view of it?
- Your team produces a genuine capability breakthrough, but its risk is not yet well understood or
  quantified. Would you delay releasing it for safety reasons, and on what basis?
- Do you support slowing down AI progress broadly for safety, even where the decision is not solely yours
  to make?
- What do you do if you hold back and a competing lab does not?
- What do you do if that choice leaves you, or your team, visibly behind?
- Separately: you are caught between a commercial deadline and a safety, reliability, or privacy concern
  you believe is real. How do you decide what to do?
- If Anthropic's equity value fell to zero, or the company faced a severe operating or financial crisis,
  how would you respond — in your own work, and in how you communicate with your manager and team?

### Critical engagement with Anthropic's public commitments

- Anthropic publishes its own safety commitments, including a scaling policy that sets out when it will
  restrict or pause deploying a model that has become more capable. Which specific part of a commitment
  like that do you agree with, and why?
- Which specific part would you change — tighten, loosen, or argue against entirely — and what would that
  change trade away?
- Have you engaged with Anthropic's own public reasoning about AI safety and its approach to training a
  model's values? What do you actually think of it?

### Values stories

- Tell me about a time someone persuaded you to change a position you had genuinely held and argued for —
  what changed your mind?
- Tell me about a working relationship that broke down, and how you repaired it.
- Tell me about a time you raised a safety, reliability, or privacy concern that slowed down or blocked a
  launch.
- Tell me about a time you disagreed with a decision made by someone with authority over you, and pushed
  back on it.
- Tell me about a mistake you made where you were the one who was wrong, and how you handled it.
- Tell me about a moral dilemma you actually faced — two obligations pulling in different directions —
  and how you decided between them.
- Tell me about something you did that you now judge to have been wrong, though it did not feel that way
  then, and how you have reconciled it since.
- Tell me about a time you held to a value under real, uncomfortable pressure to abandon it.

### Character and judgment

- What has been the most personally transformative experience of your life, inside work or outside it, and
  what changed in how you operate afterward?
- Tell me about something you keep doing purely because you care about it, with no practical payoff.
- Name the task you enjoy least — then, once the interviewer says they dislike it too, persuade them to do
  it anyway.
- What is the most complex situation, technical or otherwise, that you have handled?

## Reference solution

<summary>Show the reference solution</summary>

The round can run as a direct sequence of the questions above, or as an open-ended conversation that never
names AI safety at all; either way, the same five groups are what gets assessed, so prepare the same
material regardless of how the conversation opens. Pace yourself inside the 55 minutes: aim for two to
three minutes per story, with a clear arc from the situation to your action to what you took from it, and
let a follow-up draw out more rather than front-loading every detail.

### Motivation and mission

What it probes: whether your answer to "why Anthropic" and "why AI safety" is backed by something specific
to you — a value you can name and a piece of your own history that actually supports it — rather than a
sentence that would work at any AI company, and whether "what could Anthropic do better" gets a real,
considered answer instead of a complaint or agreement with everything.

Skeleton: name one specific value Anthropic states for itself, say concretely why you hold that value
independent of Anthropic — you should be able to name when you first noticed you cared about it — give one
example from your own life or work, from before you considered working there, where you acted on it, and
connect it to a specific, verifiable piece of your work history: a project, a decision, or the field you
moved into and why. Treat "what would make you leave" and "what could Anthropic do better" as calibration
checks rather than loyalty tests: name one real condition under which you would leave — a change in the
work itself, not a vague "if things got bad" — and one specific, defensible tension you see in how the
company operates, for example between how fast it ships and how much safety evaluation that pace leaves
room for, stated as a tradeoff rather than a grievance.

Most common way to lose points: an answer general enough to fit any AI lab ("the mission is important",
"AGI is exciting"), a value cited with no example of living it, or a "what could Anthropic do better" that
is either agreement dressed up as an answer or ordinary product feedback with no connection to values,
safety, or how the company operates. Fix: before the round, write down the one value and the one piece of
personal evidence for it, and one specific, real critique stated as what it costs to fix rather than as a
complaint.

### Hypothetical dilemmas

What it probes: whether your reasoning stays consistent as each follow-up changes one fact. There is no
single correct verdict on whether to delay; what is being assessed is a decision process you can name and
keep applying as the scenario is pushed further.

Skeleton: answer the opening risk question in two or three sentences — one mechanism you actually take most
seriously, one observation that would change your view. Then, before the first version of the breakthrough
scenario, state your actual decision criteria — for example, how reversible the potential harm is, how
quickly it would become visible, and whether a narrower release could capture some of the benefit while
more evidence is gathered — rather than jumping straight to "delay" or "ship." For each follow-up, name
explicitly which fact changed and how it moves you against those same criteria, instead of picking a new
answer from scratch:

- If you support slowing down broadly: separate whether slowing down is the right call from whether it
  changes what a competitor does. A safety practice you would take unilaterally does not stop being correct
  just because it fails to change the outcome industry-wide; say so, and name what you would actually do to
  make it count beyond your own team, such as pushing for external commitments or shared evaluation work.
- If a competitor does not slow down: say plainly that this does not by itself change whether the original
  action was right, but that it does change which options are open to you — from unilateral caution toward
  advocating for coordination you do not control alone.
- If that leaves you or your team visibly behind: distinguish behind on a competitive metric from ceding
  the field to less careful actors, name which one you are actually worried about, and what you would do
  about it operationally, in your own words, rather than an abstract "we would catch up."
- For the commercial-deadline-versus-safety version: ask what you would need to know first — what the
  actual risk is, what is really driving the deadline, and whether it can move or the release can be
  narrowed — then propose an option that serves both constraints, worked out with whoever owns the safety
  or privacy review rather than decided by you alone. If you are still asked to cross a line you consider
  real, escalate through a named channel, tell whoever asked that you are doing so, and leave a written
  record of the risk, the alternative you proposed, and who signed off on proceeding anyway.
- For the equity-to-zero version: separate what you can actually assess right now — the effect on your
  current work and any near-term operational risk — from what you cannot yet know, communicate early and
  honestly with your manager rather than deciding alone, and be honest that your own tolerance for the risk
  is a legitimate part of your answer rather than performing indifference to it.

Most common way to lose points: giving a single fixed verdict ("I would always delay" or "I would never
delay") and defending it unchanged through every follow-up, or flipping your answer at each follow-up
without saying which fact moved you — both read as reasoning that is not actually connected to the criteria
you started with. Fix: write out, for at least the breakthrough scenario, the two or three criteria you
would actually use and the fact that would flip your decision, so that each follow-up in the round becomes
"that changes criterion X, so here is how my answer moves" rather than a new answer picked from scratch.

### Critical engagement with Anthropic's public commitments

What it probes: whether you have actually read the company's own public commitments and can hold a
specific, defensible opinion about them — including a real disagreement — rather than reciting them back
approvingly or rejecting them without being able to describe them accurately.

Skeleton: name the specific commitment you are discussing precisely enough that the interviewer can tell
you have read it, not a secondhand summary; state one part you agree with and the real tradeoff it makes,
not just that it "makes sense"; state one part you would change, and be precise about what that change
would cost, since loosening a commitment buys speed at the cost of risk and tightening one buys safety
margin at the cost of falling behind on deployment; and close by naming where you are genuinely unsure
rather than asserting a confidence you do not have.

Most common way to lose points: two opposite failures — agreeing with everything, which reads as not having
engaged with the material, and disagreeing with something you cannot describe accurately, which reads as
posturing. Fix: read the documents themselves rather than relying on secondhand summaries, and walk in with
one specific point of agreement and one specific point of disagreement, each tied to a real tradeoff rather
than an up-or-down verdict. Worth having actually read before the round:

- *Core Views on AI Safety* — Anthropic's own public explanation of its overall reasoning about AI risk and
  why it works on frontier AI at all.
- *The Responsible Scaling Policy* — Anthropic's public policy tying how capable a model is allowed to
  become to the safety and security work required alongside it.
- *Claude's Constitution* — Anthropic's public document on the values and character it aims to train into
  Claude.
- *The Long-Term Benefit Trust* — Anthropic's public description of the governance body meant to represent
  its mission independent of shareholder interest.

### Values stories

What it probes: personal evidence for eight distinct claims — persuadable; able to repair a relationship,
not just avoid or win it; will hold a safety, reliability, or privacy concern under real pressure to drop
it; can disagree with authority and say how; can admit fault rather than reframe it; can reason a moral
conflict through to a decision, not just call it hard; keeps revising judgment of past conduct rather than
fixing it where you first landed; will pay a real cost to keep a value nothing forced you to. Eight
different stories are needed, each with its own action and a verifiable result.

Skeleton: situation, your position at the time, the new information or argument that entered, what you did
in response, the result, and what you do differently now. For the persuaded story, name the actual evidence
that moved you, not seniority or insistence, and what you did afterward to act on it. For the
relationship-repair story, state your own share of the breakdown and the concrete action that rebuilt
trust, not "we talked it out." For the moral-dilemma story, name the two things in tension and what you
knowingly gave up. For the now-consider-wrong story, say what let you go through with it then and what has
actually changed since — new information, a changed standard, plain hindsight. For the pressure story, name
the real cost of holding the value: one that cost nothing proves nothing.

Most common way to lose points: reusing one all-purpose conflict story across several prompts, offering a
technical or scheduling disagreement for a question about a value, or collapsing the moral-dilemma,
admitted-mistake, and now-consider-wrong prompts into one story because all three involve being at fault.
Fix: map eight stories one-to-one to the prompts — a dilemma is a choice between two legitimate claims, a
mistake is an error you owned and corrected, something you now consider wrong is a verdict that arrived
later — and keep at least one conflict story turning on a principle, not a scheduling preference.

| Story | The failure mode specific to it |
| --- | --- |
| Persuaded and changed your mind | Credits the other person's seniority or persistence instead of the actual evidence that moved you |
| Repaired a damaged relationship | Describes the fix as one conversation, with no concrete action that rebuilt trust over time |
| Safety/reliability/privacy concern that slowed a launch | No real evidence for the risk, or no honest account of what raising it cost you |
| Disagreed with leadership | Vague about how the disagreement was actually raised, or silent on the real outcome |
| Admitted a mistake | Reframes the mistake as someone else's fault or as a miscommunication rather than a wrong call |
| Faced a moral dilemma | Calls the choice hard without naming the two competing claims |
| Something you now consider wrong | Re-justifies the original call rather than revising it |
| Upheld a value under pressure | Picks a value that cost nothing, under no real pressure |

### Character and judgment

What it probes: what has shaped you outside a single decision, whether an interest survives with no payoff,
whether you can argue live under real pushback, and how you handle scale and ambiguity with no single
right move.

Skeleton: for the transformative-experience prompt, name the before-and-after — a belief, habit, or
priority different now — not a dramatic event; a personal example counts as much as a work one. For the
no-payoff prompt, name the thing precisely and what keeps you doing it unwatched. For the disliked-task
prompt, name it honestly, then, once the interviewer claims the same dislike, change the arrangement of the
work rather than only argue for it — a shared rotation, automation, or making the task's value visible to
whoever does not see it — and only briefly say why it still has to happen. For the complex-situation
prompt, name the actual sources of complexity — independent moving parts, misaligned incentives, the
uncertainty you acted under — and what made it yours to resolve.

Most common way to lose points: an event with no stated change in belief or behavior; a no-payoff answer
that is quietly instrumental; a disliked-task answer repeating the argument that already failed in the
prompt itself; a complex-situation answer long to narrate but not actually hard to resolve. Fix: for the
disliked-task prompt, prepare a structural change — a rotation, automation, or a way of surfacing the
task's value — rather than a better argument, since repeating a failed argument is the failure itself.
