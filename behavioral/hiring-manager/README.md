## Problem

A *hiring manager* is the manager of the specific team a role would join, distinct from the recruiter who
runs the process and from the engineers who run the technical rounds. An *individual contributor* (IC) is
an engineering role without direct reports, as distinct from an engineering-manager role; several prompts
below vary by this distinction and by seniority. This is a one-on-one, conversational round with the hiring
manager, scheduled for 45 to 55 minutes; the manager moves between the groups below depending on your
background rather than following a fixed script, and the round can end once its ground is covered,
sometimes well before the scheduled time. It can sit at different points in a loop: as a phone screen ahead
of the onsite, or as the last round, scheduled after the onsite's coding, system-design and culture rounds;
placed last, it also functions as a check of fit between your background and the team's current direction,
and a loop can end there even when every earlier round went well. The manager's own register ranges from
warm and exploratory to reserved and procedural; the six groups below are what the round covers under
either register. Bring, for each group, a story with your own specific role and a result you can state as a
number or an observable change; where the loop also includes a separate project deep-dive presentation,
keep the project, the numbers and the decisions you describe consistent between the two rounds.

### Past impact

- Walk through the project you consider your most impactful, and state specifically what you personally
  did in it, separate from what the team around you did.
- Describe the most complex project you have shipped: a difficulty you anticipated going in, and one that
  only became visible once the work was already underway.
- For senior ICs and staff-level candidates: describe how your impact reaches beyond the team you sit on
  directly.

### Prioritisation and ownership

- Describe the framework you use to decide between technical debt and feature work when both compete for
  the same engineering time.
- State whether a project you led carried a hard deadline, and describe how that deadline shaped the
  decisions you made.
- Describe a piece of work you deliberately chose not to do, or chose to cut, and describe a separate time
  you took on a piece of work outside the scope you were formally assigned.
- Describe a project that failed to reach its goal: its actual impact, what you learned from it, and what
  you would do differently if you ran it again.

### Collaboration

- Describe a project where you worked closely with a partner outside engineering — product, research,
  policy, design, data, or another non-engineering function.
- Describe a cross-functional collaboration that did not go well, and how it was resolved.
- Describe a disagreement with a peer or with your own manager, and how it was settled.
- Describe a time you held a view different from someone else's and persuaded them by changing your own
  approach, rather than by repeating your original argument.
- Describe a time you influenced a roadmap or a set of priorities that you did not own; for individual
  contributors, this can be a time you led an effort without formal authority over the people involved in
  it.

### Growth and mentorship

- Describe someone you helped grow, and the outcome for them.
- Describe a piece of feedback you received that changed how you work.
- Describe how you get up to speed in an area that is new to you, quickly.

### Team fit and working style

- Give a short account of your current work, and how you want your career to develop over the next few
  years.
- State what kind of team and what kind of problems you are looking for next, and why.
- Describe how you operate when a piece of work has no clear scope yet, or the goal itself is still
  unsettled.
- Describe how much you validate by default before moving to the next step of a piece of work, and how
  that changes under a deadline.
- For senior ICs and engineering managers: state the scope, the headcount and the objectives and key
  results (OKRs) behind a leadership claim you make elsewhere in the round, and describe a conflict you had
  with a peer leader.

### Your questions for the manager

- The round reserves time near its end for you to ask the hiring manager questions of your own about the
  team, the role, or how the group works.
- This is the one part of the round where the direction of the conversation is yours to set.

## Reference solution

<summary>Show the reference solution</summary>

Before the round, ask your recruiter which team the hiring manager leads, and whether it is the team the
role would place you on. When it is, bring the version of each story that carries the most detail specific
to that team's actual problems, and expect deeper follow-ups on the specific decisions inside your top
project. Prepare two stories for each of the five content groups below, each with your own specific action
and a result stated as a number or an observable change, and rehearse a version of each short enough to
deliver in about ninety seconds, since the conversation can move quickly between groups depending on where
your background naturally leads it.

### Past impact

What it probes: whether you can name your own specific contribution to a result, separate from what the
team produced together, and whether that result is something you can point to rather than assert; for the
complex-project prompt, whether you can separate a difficulty you planned for from one that only surfaced
once you were already committed.

Skeleton: name the project in one sentence — what it was, who it served — then state the result as a
number you can defend or an observable change before describing the work that led to it. Only then describe
your own actions with "I", naming the specific decisions, code, or designs that were yours even where the
project was a team effort: "the team built X; the piece I owned was Y" separates the two cleanly. For the
complex-project version, name one difficulty you expected and planned around from the start, and one that
only became visible once the work was underway, and describe what changed in your plan because of the
second one specifically.

For senior ICs and staff-level candidates, add a layer past your own individual work: how the result
reached beyond the team you sit on directly — a pattern, a library or a standard that other teams adopted;
a technical direction you argued for that changed what more than one team built next. Name the mechanism by
which the impact spread — what you actually did to make it reach other teams — not just that it eventually
helped them too.

Most common way to lose points: describing what "the team" or "we" did throughout, with no sentence that
isolates your own decisions, or a result with no number and no observable change behind it, so the
interviewer cannot tell whether the project actually mattered. Fix: rehearse the story with the result
stated in the first thirty seconds, and write out, ahead of time, the one sentence that names the part only
you did.

### Prioritisation and ownership

What it probes: whether you have an actual rule you apply when two pieces of work compete for the same
time, rather than a general belief that both matter, and, for the failure prompt, whether you can name your
own decision and what changed afterward rather than attribute the outcome to circumstance.

Skeleton for the tech-debt-versus-features framework: state the rule you actually use to compare them — for
example, weighing a feature's near-term value against the rate at which the debt is currently slowing new
work, with a threshold that shifts the answer. Give one instance where you applied it and the call that
resulted, including a case where you can state which of the two you chose to delay.

Skeleton for a deadline-driven project: name the deadline's actual source — an external commitment, a
dependency another team was waiting on, an internally chosen date — and one concrete decision the deadline
changed: a scope cut, an order of work, a risk you accepted that you would not otherwise have accepted.
State what you did to keep that risk visible rather than silent.

Skeleton for the scope-boundary stories: for work you took on outside your assignment, name the gap you
noticed, why no one else was already positioned to close it, and how you handed it back once it no longer
needed to be you — a story that ends with you still holding it indefinitely reads as a scope problem, not
an ownership story. For work you chose not to do, name the alternative you gave up, the reasoning that
ranked it below what you did instead, and what happened to it afterward.

Skeleton for a failed project: state the goal, the actual outcome against that goal, and the one decision
that mattered most to the gap between them — not a list of everything that went wrong. State what you
changed about how you work because of it, concretely enough that it would show up in your next project.

Most common way to lose points: a tech-debt answer that says both matter without a rule that actually picks
a winner in a specific case; an outside-scope story that never hands the work back; a failure story that
lists external causes without naming the one decision that was yours. Fix: write down, for each skeleton
above, the one sentence that states your rule or your decision in concrete terms before the round, so it
does not have to be invented live.

### Collaboration

What it probes: whether you can work with, and translate a trade-off for, someone whose job is not
engineering; whether a disagreement you describe is real, with an outcome that could have gone the other
way; and, for the persuasion prompt specifically, whether you changed something about your own approach
rather than simply repeating your original point until the other person agreed.

Skeleton for a cross-functional project: name the partner's actual role and the specific decision or
trade-off between you, then state how you translated the technical side into terms that person could act
on — cost, time, or risk — rather than the implementation detail itself. Close with what was decided and
who made the final call.

Skeleton for a collaboration that went wrong: name what actually broke down — a misunderstanding about
scope, or a dependency neither side flagged in time until it surfaced as a missed date — and the specific
action that fixed it, not only that the two sides talked. State what you changed about how you work with
that function afterward.

Skeleton for a disagreement with a peer or your manager: state your position and theirs, specifically
enough that a reader could see why a reasonable person could hold either one, how it was actually
settled — data, a test, or a decision by whoever owned the call — and what happened afterward to the work.

Skeleton for persuading by changing your approach: state your original argument and why repeating it was
not moving the other person, then the specific change you made — presenting the same evidence differently,
finding out what they actually needed to see, or running a small test instead of arguing the general
case — and the moment it worked. This needs a first approach that visibly failed before the change, or
there is nothing to describe changing.

Skeleton for influencing a roadmap you did not own, or leading without authority: name whose roadmap it
was, what it looked like before, the evidence you brought — data, user feedback, or a cost you could
quantify — how you got it in front of whoever could actually change it, and what changed afterward. For
the without-authority version, add who you had to bring along without being able to direct them, and what
you did instead of a direct instruction: a written proposal, or a small group you convened.

| Story | The failure mode specific to it |
| --- | --- |
| Cross-functional project | Repeats technical detail instead of translating it, or claims "good communication" with no decision behind it |
| Collaboration that went wrong | Names no specific repair, only that the two sides eventually talked |
| Disagreement with a peer or manager | States only your own position, and skips how it was actually settled |
| Persuaded by changing your approach | The "change" is the same argument said more patiently, not a different approach |
| Roadmap influence or led without authority | No evidence named, and no account of whether anything actually changed |

### Growth and mentorship

What it probes: whether you can describe someone else's growth as a specific sequence of changes with a
real outcome, not a general claim that you helped them; and whether feedback you received actually changed
a habit, not just that you accepted it.

Skeleton for the mentorship story: name the person's starting point — a skill gap, or a kind of task they
had not yet owned independently — what you specifically did (how you chose their tasks, and what you
explained versus what you let them work out), and the outcome, with a marker of the change: they now own a
kind of task without you. Separate what was your doing from what was their own effort.

Skeleton for feedback that changed how you work: name the specific feedback, who gave it and in what
setting, why your first reaction to it was not simple agreement — this is what makes the story about growth
rather than politeness — and the concrete change you made afterward: a habit, a step you now take, a
question you now ask before doing something you used to skip. State how you know the change stuck: a later
instance where you caught yourself doing it differently.

Skeleton for learning a new area quickly: name the area and why you needed it fast, then the specific
method you used to get productive in it — a fixed, small set of source material read end to end rather
than skimming broadly, and a small thing built before attempting the real one — and the point at which you
were contributing independently, with a rough timeline.

Most common way to lose points: a mentorship story with no milestone, which can only say the person
"improved"; a feedback story where the feedback and the resulting change are both generic ("communicate
more"); a fast-learning story that lists resources with no moment where you were tested against the new
material and found the gaps. Fix: attach one concrete, checkable moment to each of the three — the task the
mentee first did alone, the situation where you noticed yourself applying the feedback, the point where you
shipped something in the new area without help.

### Team fit and working style

What it probes: whether your account of your own goals is specific enough to check against the team you
are talking to, rather than a general career narrative, and whether you can describe your own working
style — under ambiguity, and under time pressure — concretely enough to compare against how this team
actually operates.

Skeleton for background and career direction: two or three sentences on your current work and its
throughline, then what you are looking for next stated as a property of the work — a kind of problem, a
kind of scope, a kind of system — rather than a title or a level, and why this specific team's problems
match it, named concretely enough that it would not fit an unrelated team equally well.

Skeleton for ambiguity: describe a piece of work that arrived with a goal but no scope, what you did first
to reduce that ambiguity — a conversation with whoever owned the goal, a smallest version you could build to
learn something, a written framing of the problem you circulated before starting — and the point at which
the work had a scope you and others could actually plan against.

Skeleton for pace: state how much you validate before moving to the next step by default — a design
reviewed before code, a small test before a full rollout, a number checked before a claim — and name the
one thing you cut first under a real deadline, and the one thing you do not cut regardless of the deadline,
with the reason it stays.

For senior ICs and engineering managers, be ready to state, for a leadership claim you have already made
elsewhere in the round, its actual scope, its headcount, and which OKRs you personally owned versus
contributed to, and to describe a specific conflict with a peer leader — over scope or a conflicting
priority — including how it was resolved and by whom.

Most common way to lose points: a career-direction answer generic enough to fit any team at any company; an
ambiguity story where the reduction step is skipped and the story jumps straight to execution; a pace
answer with nothing named as non-negotiable, which reads as someone who cuts anything under pressure. Fix:
name the team's actual problem area in your career-direction answer rather than a property any team would
claim to have ("impact", "growth"), and write down, ahead of time, the one thing you do not cut.

### When the manager gives little back

A short answer with no follow-up does not by itself mean the previous one landed badly: keep the shape of a
STAR answer — situation, your specific action, the result — rather than expanding it to fill a silence.
When you cannot tell whether more depth is wanted, say so directly instead of guessing: "I can go deeper
into the technical decisions here, or move on to the next story — which is more useful?" When the round
sits as the last gate in the loop, end each story with a sentence tying it to the kind of work this team
actually does, rather than leaving that connection for the manager to infer.

### Questions worth asking the hiring manager

What it probes: whether the questions you ask are specific to this team and this point in your process, and
whether they show you have actually thought about what you would be joining rather than filling reserved
time.

Skeleton: prepare two or three questions ahead of time, weighted toward what only this specific manager can
answer — not something a public source already states. Strong categories: what the team is working on
right now, and why that is the current priority over the alternatives; what the role would own in its
first few months, concretely enough to compare against your own skills; how the manager would describe the
gap between the team today and where it needs to be.

Most common way to lose points: no questions at all, or a question answerable from public material, both
of which read as not having prepared for this specific conversation. Fix: write your two or three questions
down before the round, each one naming something you could only learn from this manager.
